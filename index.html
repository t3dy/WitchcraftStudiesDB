Three Historiographical Traditions

Stuart Clark

Intellectual History

Demonology as coherent theological system. Witch prosecution understood through the rationality of learned theory, not irrational panic. Thinking with Demons (1997) remains the definitive statement.

Ronald Hutton

Social History

Cunning folk as legitimate practitioners. Demographic and community analysis of how accusations emerged from social tensions and economic conflict between neighbors.

Carlo Ginzburg

Microhistory

Interrogation analysis revealing how torture shaped confessions. Voice recovery for accused persons through close reading — his benandanti discovery transformed the field.

Eight Historiographical Principles

1. Provenance on every claim. Every substantive claim carries source_method + confidence rating.
2. Rationality within framework. Witch prosecution was rational within its theological premises (Clark).
3. Actor/Analyst distinction. Practitioners' own language versus modern analytical categories kept separate.
4. Regional variation as primary. No single "European witch hunt" — each region had distinct patterns.
5. Microhistorical attention to individuals. Named persons, not just statistics.
6. Material grounding. What people actually did, not just what they were accused of.
7. Intellectual history seriously. Demonological texts treated as serious theological documents.
8. Ambiguity preserved. Historiographical disagreement stated explicitly in every entry.
