These essays extend beyond the database entries to offer longer-form analysis of specific cases, the scholarly frameworks used to understand them, and — where relevant — the films, novels, and stage works that have brought historical witch trials to modern audiences. Each essay situates its subject within the three main interpretive traditions of WitchcraftStudiesDB: Clark's intellectual history, Hutton's social history, and Ginzburg's microhistory.

Case Studies

Scotland · 1590–1597 · Crown Prosecution

The North Berwick Witch Trials and James VI's Daemonologie

Scotland's most sensational witch prosecution — a king who attended interrogations himself, and the royal treatise that shaped a generation

The 1590–91 North Berwick trials alleged a diabolical conspiracy to murder King James VI by raising storms to sink his ship. James attended the interrogations personally, was convinced by Agnes Sampson's whispered wedding-night words, and went on to write Daemonologie (1597) as a direct refutation of Reginald Scot's skepticism. The essay examines the torture record, the Bothwell political dimension, and the historiographical debate between Clark, Ginzburg, and Christina Larner over what the trials actually show.

France · 1632–1634 · Possession Case

The Loudun Possessions and the Trial of Urbain Grandier

Ursuline nuns, public exorcism spectacles, a forged devil's pact, and a priest who refused to confess under torture

In 1632, Ursuline nuns at Loudun began exhibiting possession symptoms and naming parish priest Urbain Grandier as their tormentor. The ensuing exorcism sessions became public spectacles attended by thousands; the evidence included a demonstrably forged document purporting to be Grandier's signed pact with Hell. Grandier was tortured, refused to confess, and was burned alive in August 1634. Cardinal Richelieu's agents managed the prosecution throughout. The essay addresses the fraud-versus-belief question, Roper's gender-historical reading of the nuns' behavior, Clark on possession categories, and the cultural afterlife in Huxley, John Whiting, and Ken Russell.

Finland / Sweden · 1665–1668 · Film and History

Devil's Bride and the Kastelholm Witch Trials

The 2016 Finnish film, the historical prosecution it dramatizes, and where fiction parts from the record

Tulen morsian ("Bride of Fire," 2016) is set against the Kastelholm witch trials of the Swedish-governed Åland Islands — the most significant mass witch prosecution in Finnish history. The essay compares the film's narrative (centered on a jealous maidservant's false accusation) against the historical record: a cunning woman named Karin Persdotter, a judge educated in Continental demonology at Tartu, and the denunciation cascade that followed. Examines how the film depicts systemic prosecution while personalizing its origins, and situates the trials within the broader Swedish panic of the 1660s–70s.
