# Gap List — Hutton, *The Witch: A History of Fear* (2017)

## New `demonological_scholar` entries needed

- [ ] `murray-margaret-a-b1863` — Margaret Alice Murray (1863–1963), Egyptologist at University College London; wrote The Witch-Cult in Western Europe (1921) arguing early modern witches practised a surviving pre-Christian fertility religion; her thesis dominated popular understanding mid-20th c. before scholarly demolition c. 1970; indispensable negative reference point for the entire field
- [ ] `eliade-mircea-b1907` — Mircea Eliade (1907–1986), Romanian-American; professor of History of Religions at University of Chicago; defined shamanism as archaic and formerly universal technique of ecstasy in Shamanism: Archaic Techniques of Ecstasy (1951); by 1970s half of American full professors in his discipline were his pupils; influenced Ginzburg's interpretation of benandanti and Pócs's European shamanistic substratum hypothesis; himself influenced by Frazer
- [ ] `goodare-julian-b1954` — Julian Goodare (born c. 1954), Scottish historian at University of Edinburgh; leading expert on early modern Scotland; developed hypothesis of Scottish "seely wights" cult as extension of Ginzburg's approach in 2012; author of The Scottish Witch-Hunt in Context (2002) and The European Witch-Hunt (2016)

## New `demonological_concept` entries needed

- [ ] `murray-thesis-pagan-survival` — The claim, popularized by Margaret Murray but with 19th-century predecessors, that early modern witches practised a coherent religion descended from ancient paganism, centred on a horned god of fertility; tested empirically by archival research and found to be without foundation by c. 1970; historiographical landmark because its collapse forced the field to develop new frameworks (Cohn's stereotype model; Ginzburg's folk-belief model); relevant to `witches-sabbath-collective`, `ecstatic-tradition-night-journey`
- [ ] `seely-wights-scottish` — Scottish supernatural beings, neither fairies nor humans, identified by Goodare in a mid-1530s Scottish text; believed to associate with female practitioners who joined them in nocturnal flights; Goodare hypothesizes a Scottish "cult" of shamanic magical practitioners, mostly female, analogous to Ginzburg's benandanti and Henningsen's "ladies from outside"; tentative evidence only; illustrates variant of the followers-of-the-lady tradition in a Celtic-language context

- [ ] `murray-witch-cult-1921` — Margaret Murray, The Witch-Cult in Western Europe, Oxford University Press, 1921; founding text of the pagan-survival thesis; based mainly on published British primary sources; important negative reference
