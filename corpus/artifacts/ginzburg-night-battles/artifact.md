## Core Thesis (2–4 sentences)

Ginzburg argues that in the Friuli region of north-east Italy between roughly 1570 and 1650, a group of peasant practitioners called the benandanti — men and women born with the caul who believed their souls left their bodies during the Ember Days to do battle with witches in defence of the harvest — represent a stratum of genuinely popular folk belief that was gradually assimilated to the inquisitorial stereotype of diabolical witchcraft over the course of approximately eighty years. The book's methodological core is that the "gap" between inquisitors' predetermined questions and the initial testimony of the accused reveals pre-inquisitorial popular belief that preceded and resisted the overlay of learned demonology; this gap eventually closed, and by the 1640s benandanti confessed to full sabbath participation. Ginzburg further argues that the benandanti's nocturnal spirit-battles are connected to a pan-Eurasian complex of shamanistic ideas (trance, soul-flight, animal transformation, fertility magic, processions of the dead), offering a partial explanation for why witchcraft stereotypes drew on genuinely popular cultural materials. The book also demonstrates how Roman Inquisition moderating influence from Rome — particularly through the *Instructio pro formandis processibus* (c. 1620) — ultimately prevented the benandanti trials from reaching the lethal persecution scale seen in Germany or Scotland.

## Major Arguments by Chapter / Section

### Preface to the English Edition (1982)
- **Argument**: The benandanti are connected to Murray's thesis only in the first of Murray's two claims (witchcraft has roots in an ancient fertility cult), not the second (sabbath gatherings actually took place); scholars who claim the benandanti as "the only authenticated witch cult in early modern Europe" (Midelfort) misread the evidence.
- **Evidence**: Ginzburg's own rebuttal of J.B. Russell and Midelfort; correction of Cohn's claim that benandanti experiences were "all trance experiences."
- **DB relevance**: `demonological_concept/benandante-fertility-cult`; `demonological_scholar/cohn-norman-b1915`; `demonological_scholar/midelfort-hans-c-erik-b1942`.

### Preface to the Italian Edition (1966)
- **Argument**: The methodological key to recovering popular belief from inquisitorial records is the gap between the judges' presuppositions and the accused's testimony; only because the Friulian inquisitors were confused about what the benandanti were — and therefore slow to impose the sabbath template — do we have access to an otherwise invisible stratum of peasant mentality.
- **Evidence**: Contrast with the Enlightenment tradition (Hansen, Tartarotti) of treating all confessions as coerced fabrication; Ginzburg proposes that the confessions contain genuine popular content distinguishable from inquisitorial overlay.
- **DB relevance**: `demonological_concept/torture-confession-dynamic`; `demonological_concept/acculturation-thesis`; core methodological principle for all accused person entries.

### Chapter I: The Night Battles
- **Argument**: From 1575 onward, the Friuli inquisitors encountered men (and eventually women) who insisted they were not witches but *benandanti* — chosen by birth (those born with the caul), summoned by angels or older benandanti, and compelled to leave their sleeping bodies on Ember Day Thursdays to fight witches armed with sorghum stalks for the fertility of the fields.
- **Evidence**: Detailed verbatim testimonies of Paolo Gasparutto (Iassico) and Battista Moduco (Cividale), 1575–1581; witness depositions from priest Don Bartolomeo Sgabarizza, nobleman Troiano de'Attimis, and miller Pietro Rotaro; analysis of Fra Felice da Montefalco's unsuccessful attempts to force Moduco to identify the benandanti gatherings as sabbaths.
- **DB relevance**: `demonological_concept/benandante-fertility-cult`; `healer_practitioner` entries for Gasparutto and Moduco; `inquisitorial_body` entry for Aquileian Inquisition; `trial_event` for the 1575–1581 Cividale proceedings.

### Chapter I (continued): Resistance and the Inquisitorial Template
- **Argument**: The benandanti strongly resisted assimilation to the witch stereotype; Moduco and Gasparutto maintained they fought *for* God and Christ, not the devil; their captain was saluted "like soldiers to their captain" not kneeled before; they did not eat or drink at the gathering but drank from wine cellars on their way home. The inquisitors were equally trapped in their predetermined schema: they persistently asked about sabbath orgies, anointing, and pacts.
- **Evidence**: Fra Felice's direct confrontation with Moduco: "How could you make yourself believe that these were God's works?"; Moduco's insistence that the captain "promised I would go to paradise."
- **DB relevance**: `demonological_concept/torture-confession-dynamic`; `demonological_concept/acculturation-thesis`; `demonological_scholar/ginzburg-carlo-b1939`.

### Chapter I (continued): Later 16th Century — Expansion and Variants
- **Argument**: By 1591, the cowherd Menichino della Nota (Latisana) provides a variant account — initiation by an older benandante while walking one winter night, not by an angel; fighting "for the faith" in the Valle di Josafat; soul leaving the body "like smoke"; body must not be turned over during the absence or the soul cannot return. A woman, Maria Panzona, provides evidence that female benandanti participated in similar battles but in the form of butterflies or cats.
- **Evidence**: Interrogation of Menichino, October 1591; preliminary mention of Maria Panzona, tried 1619.
- **DB relevance**: `healer_practitioner` entries for Menichino; `demonological_concept/ecstatic-tradition-night-journey`; `demonological_concept/benandante-fertility-cult`.

### Chapter II: The Processions of the Dead
- **Argument**: A second, distinct folk tradition in the Friuli — the ability to see and converse with the dead — is documented alongside the benandanti and eventually linked to them; practitioners like Anna la Rossa of Udine (1581) claimed a special ability conferred by birth ("under that sign") to see the dead and relay their messages to the living; the caul, birthmark of the benandanti, is also identified as the source of this necromantic gift, suggesting both traditions share a common root in beliefs about the "outer soul" and the world of prematurely deceased spirits.
- **Evidence**: Trial of Anna la Rossa, 1581–1582; trial of Aquilina (wife of a tailor, Udine, 1582); parallel traditions in Denmark, Lucca, and Bergamo; Grana of Villa Marzana, Modenese Inquisition 1601.
- **DB relevance**: `demonological_concept/ecstatic-tradition-night-journey`; `demonological_concept/followers-of-the-lady-night-ride`; `healer_practitioner` entries for Anna la Rossa and Aquilina; concept entry needed for "processions of the dead."

### Chapter II (continued): Wild Hunt Connection
- **Argument**: The witches against whom the benandanti fight are structurally identified with the wandering souls of the prematurely dead (malandanti), revealing that the underlying mythological complex is a version of the Wild Hunt / Furious Army; at Lucca and Bergamo, healers cured those "afflicted by the dead" or by "shadows," confirming that fear of wandering pre-mortem souls was geographically widespread; the connection between benandanti and shamans, suggested by Mircea Eliade, is supported by the Livonian werewolf parallels (also good walkers who fought witches on behalf of the harvest, who under inquisitorial pressure also became associated with negative wolf-traits).
- **Evidence**: Gasparutto's description of the *malandante* (benandante who does not return in time); clerici vagantes and the "Furious Horde"; Peucer on Livonian benevolent werewolves; Grana of Villa Marzana on wandering souls of prematurely dead.
- **DB relevance**: `demonological_concept/ecstatic-tradition-night-journey`; `demonological_concept/followers-of-the-lady-night-ride`; concept entry needed for "malandante / processions of the dead."

### Chapter III: The Benandanti between Inquisitors and Witches
- **Argument**: From the 1580s to 1610s, inquisitors gradually succeeded in making individual benandanti partially identify their gatherings with sabbaths; the process was not one of simple coercion but of genuine confusion and cross-contamination, as benandanti tried to maintain their identity while accepting elements of the inquisitorial schema; Maria Panzona's 1619 trial is the key case: she oscillated between benandante and witch identities across two phases of interrogation, reverting to her original benandante identity when the threat of torture was lifted.
- **Evidence**: Maria Panzona's interrogations at Latisana (early phase, partial sabbath elements) and Venice (later phase, reversion to benandante identity under non-coercive conditions); her lawyer's instruction to deny everything ("he told me I should deny everything, and say it was not true").
- **DB relevance**: `demonological_concept/torture-confession-dynamic`; `demonological_concept/acculturation-thesis`; `healer_practitioner` entry for Maria Panzona; `trial_event` for her case.

### Chapter IV: The Benandanti at the Sabbath
- **Argument**: By the 1620s–1640s, Giovanni Sion (1623) and then Michele Soppe (1649) provide the first fully realized sabbath confessions from the Friuli — complete with devil-worship, apostasy, and infanticide; this transformation was not imposed by torture in these cases but had been so thoroughly internalized by that generation that Sion "spontaneously" confessed a full sabbath; yet both Sion and Soppe still preserve vestigial benandante elements (Soppe uses a fennel stalk when calling the devil; benandanti do not kiss the devil's arse at the sabbath), showing that the old myth was "too deeply engrained to fade away" in a few years.
- **Evidence**: Giovanni Sion's 1623 confession to Don Pietro Martire da Verona; Sion's interrogation by Fra Ludovico da Gualdo, Cividale, 29 April 1623; Michele Soppe's 1649 proceedings before Fra Giulio Missini; Cardinal Francesco Barberini's 1649 letter requiring verification of infanticide allegations; the *Instructio pro formandis processibus* (c. 1620) moderating Roman Inquisition policy.
- **Evidence 2**: Language barrier as additional obstacle: Fra Giulio Missini, a native of Orvieto, required an interpreter to question Friulian-dialect-speaking Soppe mid-seventeenth century.
- **DB relevance**: `demonological_concept/witches-sabbath-collective`; `demonological_concept/acculturation-thesis`; `trial_event` for Sion and Soppe cases; `inquisitorial_body` for Roman Congregation of Holy Office.

## Key Passages

### For: `demonological_concept/acculturation-thesis`
> "Thus, the goal of the present research is to document and build upon the approach to the question originally developed by Hansen. What is new about this effort, even if limited in scope, is the contribution that it can make to our understanding of the significance and nature of popular witchcraft, as distinguished from the learned conceptions of inquisitorial origin."
*Why this passage matters: Defines Ginzburg's methodological project — recovering popular belief from beneath the inquisitorial overlay — the defining move of his microhistorical approach.*

### For: `demonological_concept/benandante-fertility-cult`
> "Gasparutto said that 'on Thursdays during the Ember Days of the year they were forced to go with these witches to many places, such as Cormons, in front of the church at Iassico, and even into the countryside about Verona,' where 'they fought, played, leaped about, and rode various animals, and did different things among themselves; and . . . the women beat the men who were with them with sorghum stalks, while the men had only bunches of fennel.'"
*Why this passage matters: The foundational primary description of benandanti activity — Ember Day battles with gendered weapons — directly relevant to the concept entry and any healer_practitioner entries for Gasparutto.*

### For: `demonological_concept/torture-confession-dynamic`
> "The gap between the questions of the judges and the confessions of the accused which was gradually reduced only in the course of decades. To his credit, P. Burke has seen in the use I have made of this gap a device by which, through broader application, the student of popular culture may circumvent the limitations inherent in judicial sources."
*Why this passage matters: The "gap" is Ginzburg's core methodological concept; this passage is the most concise statement of it and should be referenced in the torture-confession-dynamic concept entry.*

### For: `demonological_concept/ecstatic-tradition-night-journey`
> "Such phenomena as trances, journeys into the beyond astride animals or in the form of animals (wolves or, as in the Friuli, butterflies and mice) to recover seed grain or to assure the fertility of the land, and as we will note shortly, participation in processions for the dead (which procured prophetic and visionary powers for the benandanti) form a coherent pattern which immediately evokes the rites of the shamans."
*Why this passage matters: Ginzburg's own statement of the shamanism connection; usefully paired with Hutton's critique of its Eliade-derived foundations.*

### For: `healer_practitioner/panzona-maria-fl1619` (or `accused_person`)
> "I have never abjured my faith in Jesus Christ, but I did say that the other witches abjured it . . . (the accused women) are witches, and I know this because I was with them in the form of a cat, and they also were in the form of cats: they to injure and I to protect."
*Why this passage matters: Maria Panzona's final restatement of her benandante identity after retracting her sabbath confession — one of the most vivid examples of how firmly popular belief persisted beneath inquisitorial pressure.*

### For: `demonological_concept/witches-sabbath-collective` and `acculturation-thesis`
> "From the battles waged by the ancient benandanti in defence of Christ's faith, we have arrived at the apostasy, to which the benandante Michele Soppe admitted, of that very same faith. The original myth was even symbolically rejected since the trampled cross was made out of branches of viburnum, in imitation of the crosses which were placed in fields during the processions of the Rogation Days to ward off storms, once a symbol of fertility appropriated by benandanti such as Gasparutto as a weapon in the battles they fought in their dreams against witches."
*Why this passage matters: Ginzburg's most explicit statement of the total transformation — from Christian fertility defenders to apostates — and how even the symbolic inversion of the viburnum cross confirms the original fertility meaning was still accessible to him as evidence.*
