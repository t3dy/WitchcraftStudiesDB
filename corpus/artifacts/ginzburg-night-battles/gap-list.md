- [ ] `gasparutto-paolo-fl1575` — Paolo Gasparutto, benandante of Iassico village, Friuli; appeared before inquisitor March 1575; healed bewitched persons and divined through knowledge of nocturnal spirit-battles; central figure of Chapter I; described Ember Day battles (fennel stalks vs. sorghum stalks, animal riding, fighting for harvest fertility); sentenced to six months' penance 1581; source: *Night Battles* pp. 1–52
- [ ] `moduco-battista-fl1575` — Battista Moduco, public crier of Cividale, Friuli; interrogated 1575–1581 before Fra Felice da Montefalco; resisted inquisitorial assimilation more effectively than Gasparutto; insisted benandanti fought for God not devil; his captain "promised he would go to paradise"; sentenced six months' penance 1581; source: *Night Battles* pp. 1–52
- [ ] `menichino-della-nota-fl1591` — Menichino della Nota, cowherd of Latisana, Friuli; 1591 testimony before inquisitor's commissioner; introduced variant initiation account (by living benandante Giambattista Tamburlino while walking on a winter night, not by angel); described Valle di Josafat as battle-site; soul left body "like smoke"; wife had believed him dead in his bed; source: *Night Battles* pp. 99–108
- [ ] `anna-la-rossa-fl1581` — Anna la Rossa (widow of Domenico Artichi), Udine, Friuli; tried by Fra Felice da Montefalco 1581–1582; claimed ability to see and communicate with the dead, charging small fees (five soldi) for her services; case left unfinished (she absconded to Spilimbergo); structurally connected by Ginzburg to the benandante complex through the caul and night-journeying; source: *Night Battles* pp. 53–68
- [ ] `aquilina-udine-fl1582` — Aquilina (wife of a tailor, no surname given), Udine, Friuli; 1582 inquisitorial proceedings; operated as service magician ("profession of seeing"), earning reportedly 100–200 ducats per year from a "great multitude of people"; fled prosecution to Latisana; eventually interrogated but case unresolved; refused the label of witch; source: *Night Battles* pp. 62–67
- [ ] `panzona-maria-fl1619` — Maria Panzona, female benandante; tried at Latisana and then Venice, 1619; travelled to nocturnal battles in the form of butterfly and later cat; "I go to protect, they to injure"; oscillated between benandante and witch identities across two phases of interrogation; epileptic; lawyer advised her to deny everything; ultimately reverted to benandante identity after fear of torture passed; pronounced mildly suspect of heresy, condemned to three years imprisonment and perpetual banishment from Latisana; source: *Night Battles* pp. 107–132
- [ ] `sion-giovanni-fl1623` — Giovanni Sion, benandante of Moimacco (servant in a noble household of Cividale); 1623; seduced into attending the sabbath by a witch named Gerolamo of Villalta (who anointed himself with oil and rode a lion); provided first fully realized sabbath confession from the Friuli (including apostasy elements); his case was brought to the inquisitor by Benedictine Don Pietro Martire da Verona; interrogated by Fra Ludovico da Gualdo, 29 April 1623; source: *Night Battles* pp. 128–141
- [ ] `soppe-michele-fl1649` — Michele Soppe, last significant benandante of the Friuli; 1648–1649 proceedings before Fra Giulio Missini; confessed apostasy (trampling a viburnum cross), infanticide (three children including a nephew, by sucking blood as a cat through "diabolical arts"), and regular diabolical sabbath attendance; yet still used a fennel stalk when calling the devil and maintained benandanti did not kiss the devil's arse; too poor for a private lawyer; Roman Congregation (Cardinal Barberini) intervened to require medical verification before sentencing; case exemplifies the completion of the acculturation process and the moderating role of Rome; source: *Night Battles* pp. 132–152

## New `demonological_concept` entries needed

- [ ] `processions-of-the-dead-friulian` — HYBRID concept (ACTOR from popular perspective): the belief, documented alongside benandante tradition in the Friuli, that certain persons (often caul-born) could see and converse with the wandering souls of the prematurely dead, relay their last wishes, and even heal those "afflicted by the dead" or "shadows." Ginzburg argues this tradition shares a common mythological root with the benandanti — both involve nocturnal soul-journeying by caul-born individuals into a world of the dead — representing a "funereal" variant of the same underlying complex that the benandante agrarian battles represent in their "agrarian" form. Sources: Anna la Rossa (Udine 1581); Aquilina (Udine 1582); parallel traditions in Denmark, Lucca, Bergamo; Grana of Villa Marzana (Modenese Inquisition 1601)
- [ ] `malandante` — ACTOR concept (Ginzburg, *Night Battles*): the soul of a benandante who does not return to its body before dawn, or who commits a crime during the nocturnal journey; condemned to wander like the prematurely dead ("until the time appointed for that body to die, like an evil, hostile presence"); linguistically the inversion of *benandante* (good-doer); "these malandanti devour little children"; reveals that the distinction between benandanti and witches maps onto a deeper popular distinction between souls properly returned to their bodies and souls of the prematurely dead; concept was articulated by Gasparutto himself

## New `scholarly_text` entries needed

- [ ] `hansen-zauberwahn-1900` — J. Hansen, *Zauberwahn, Inquisition und Hexenprozess im Mittelalter*, 1900; documented how the diabolical witchcraft image (pact, sabbat, profanation of sacraments) was developed between mid-13th and mid-15th centuries by theologians and inquisitors; foundational to Ginzburg's methodological starting point; not yet in DB
- [ ] `instructio-processibus-1620` — *Instructio pro formandis processibus in causis strigarum sortilegiorum et maleficiorum*, c. 1620 (circulated in manuscript; published 1655 by Cesare Carena of Cremona); anonymous; written in the circles of the Roman Congregation of the Holy Office; required extreme caution, medical verification, and avoidance of suggestive questioning in witchcraft prosecutions; directly applied in the Michele Soppe case (1649); explains why Italian peninsula had virtually no lethal witchcraft condemnations after mid-17th century; should be a `primary_source` entry
- [ ] `peucer-commentarius-1553` — Caspar Peucer, *Commentarius de praecipuis divinationum generibus*, 1553; contains account of Livonian werewolves who "boasted that they kept witches away" — benevolent lycanthropy parallel to benandanti that under inquisitorial pressure also became associated with negative wolf-attributes; used by Ginzburg to suggest "real, not merely analogical" connection between benandanti and shamanism
