## Core Thesis (2–4 sentences)

Clark argues that demonological writing — the body of learned texts on witchcraft produced between roughly 1430 and 1720 — was not irrational aberration but a coherent intellectual system whose internal logic depended on the same modes of thought (scientific, historical, religious, political) that animated early modern European culture as a whole. Witchcraft was not a positive concept with independent content but a fundamentally relational one: it derived its meaning from a pervasive cultural logic of binary opposition and inversion, making the witch the necessary negative pole of every significant social and moral category. Demonology was therefore a *resource* for thinking through problems that originated in natural philosophy, eschatology, pastoral theology, and political theory — hence the Lévi-Straussian title *Thinking with Demons*. Clark explicitly rejects any direct causal link between demonological texts and the incidence of witch trials, insisting that the two must be studied as partially independent phenomena.

## Major Arguments by Chapter / Section

### Part I: Language (Chapters 1–9, pp. 3–186)

#### Ch. 1 — Witchcraft and Language (p. 3)
- **Argument**: Witchcraft was a Saussurean sign whose meaning was constituted negatively and relationally, not by positive reference to real acts; anti-realist philosophy of language is the appropriate framework for studying demonological texts.
- **Evidence**: Linguistic theory (Saussure, Derrida, MacIntyre on rationality); critique of realist, rationalist, and social-science approaches to witchcraft.
- **DB relevance**: `demonological_concept` entries; foundational methodological note for all entries.

#### Ch. 2 — Festivals and Sabbats (p. 12)
- **Argument**: The witches' sabbat was intelligible to early modern readers because it was structured as a systematic inversion of festival and social ritual; the sabbat's meaning depended entirely on familiarity with what it inverted.
- **Evidence**: Hans Baldung Grien drawing (1514); sabbat descriptions by Rémy, Grillando, DʼAnania, Guazzo, de Lancre, Elich, Maldonado; parallel with charivari, carnival, court masque.
- **DB relevance**: `witches-sabbath-collective`, `inversion-principle`; entries for Rémy, Guazzo, de Lancre.

#### Ch. 3 — Dual Classification (p. 47)
- **Argument**: The recourse to binary oppositions in early modern high culture was pervasive and drew on Aristotle's four types of opposition (Categoriae); witchcraft fitted naturally into this pre-existing framework of dual classification.
- **Evidence**: Aristotle's Categoriae, Topica, Metaphysica; Renaissance dialectic textbooks; Needham's anthropology of binary opposition.
- **DB relevance**: `inversion-principle`; `contrariety` concept (new entry needed).

#### Ch. 4 — Contrariety (p. 61)
- **Argument**: Contrariety was a universal principle of intelligibility — a statement both about language and about the actual constitution of the world — that made witchcraft cognizable as the necessary opposite of all positive moral and natural goods.
- **Evidence**: Augustine's theodicy; Aristotelian physics; Renaissance rhetoric (antitheton/contentio); early modern theology.
- **DB relevance**: `inversion-principle`; concept entry for `contrariety-universal-principle` (new).

#### Ch. 5 — Inversion (p. 92)
- **Argument**: Inversion as a characterization of disorder was not merely rhetorical but expressed a whole metaphysic; the comparison of prince/tyrant was the archetypal political application of the inversionary logic that also produced the witch.
- **Evidence**: Political theory on tyranny; demonological comparisons between God/Devil, church/sabbat.
- **DB relevance**: `inversion-principle`, `diabolical-conspiracy-collective-guilt`.

#### Ch. 6 — The Devil, God's Ape (p. 106)
- **Argument**: The devil's defining characteristic as God's ape (simia Dei) guaranteed that demonic witchcraft made sense as a systematic parody of divine order; this completed the textual construction of the witch within a framework of opposition.
- **Evidence**: Patristic and scholastic tradition; demonological writers linking devil to carnival and court masque inversion.
- **DB relevance**: New concept entry needed: `devil-as-gods-ape`.

#### Ch. 7 — Witchcraft and Wit-Craft (p. 124)
- **Argument**: The rhetorical form of demonological argument — especially antitheton (contentio) — was constitutive of its content; demonological writing participated in the standard literate conventions of Renaissance wit and paradox.
- **Evidence**: Ciceronian rhetoric; paradox literature; James VI's Daemonologie.
- **DB relevance**: `james-vi-scotland-b1566`; analytical frame for all `scholarly_text` entries.

#### Ch. 8 — Women and Witchcraft (p. 138)
- **Argument**: Witches were culturally constructed as female because women and witches were made intelligible through the same hierarchical oppositions (reason/passion, order/disorder, virtue/vice); the feminization of the witch was an effect of binary logic, not a free-standing social fact.
- **Evidence**: Demonological characterizations of women; Kramer's Malleus; comparative anthropology (Beidelman); feminist history (Barstow, Roper) critiqued.
- **DB relevance**: `kramer-heinrich-b1430`, `sprenger-jakob-b1436`; concept `witch-gender-binary-construction` (new).

#### Ch. 9 — Unstable Meanings (p. 171)
- **Argument**: Demonology's very reliance on binary opposition created inherent instabilities — the demonic could always be mistaken for the divine, the miraculous for the magical — that generated permanent interpretive anxiety.
- **Evidence**: Theological debates on discernment of spirits; Ginzburg's *Ecstasies* (engaged but partly countered: sabbat's symbolic inversion framework is more powerful than folkloric origins thesis).
- **DB relevance**: `ginzburg-carlo-b1939`; `discernment-of-spirits` concept (new).

### Part II: Science (Chapters 10–19, pp. 187–375)

#### Ch. 10 — Witchcraft and Science (p. 187)
- **Argument**: The dominant questions of demonological literature were not moral but natural-philosophical — could witches actually achieve attributed effects? — making demonology a species of natural inquiry, not a departure from it.
- **Evidence**: Full spectrum from Nider and Alphonsus de Spina to Glanvill, Bekker, Thomasius; Canon episcopi on night-flying as illusion.
- **DB relevance**: `nider-johannes-b1380`, `bekker-balthasar-b1634`, `thomasius-christian-b1655`, `glanvill-joseph-b1636`; `natural-demonic-magic-distinction`.

#### Ch. 11 — The Devil in Nature (p. 199)
- **Argument**: Devils were accorded vast but still finite natural powers (drawing on their pre-Fall angelic capacities); this grounded demonology within natural philosophy — the devil operated in and through nature, not above it.
- **Evidence**: Scholastic angelology; Protestant and Catholic treatments of demonic causation.
- **DB relevance**: `preternatural-category`.

#### Ch. 12 — The Causes of Witchcraft (p. 220)
- **Argument**: Applying a Foucauldian framework, Clark shows that the witch's "visibility" depended entirely on the classificatory grid of pre-modern natural philosophy; demonological debate produced an elaborate causal taxonomy of natural/preternatural/supernatural.
- **Evidence**: Foucault's *Birth of the Clinic* (as heuristic); early modern natural philosophical texts; argument against Trevor-Roper's "hysterical" dismissal and against liberal historiography (Lecky, Burr).
- **DB relevance**: `preternatural-category`; `trevor-roper-hr-b1914` (new scholar entry needed).

#### Ch. 13 — Believers and Sceptics (p. 240)
- **Argument**: The division of demonologists into "believers" and "sceptics" is a false binary imposed by modern rationalism; the actual literature occupied a vast, nuanced middle ground where different claims were evaluated on a case-by-case basis using natural-philosophical criteria.
- **Evidence**: Bodin (extreme believer), Scot (extreme sceptic), and hundreds of intermediate texts; Molitor, Erastus, Weyer.
- **DB relevance**: `scot-reginald-b1538`, `weyer-johann-b1516`, `bodin-jean-b1530`; `believers-sceptics-spectrum` concept (new).

#### Ch. 14 — Natural Magic (p. 262)
- **Argument**: Natural magic — a legitimate branch of Renaissance natural philosophy — was the fourth causal possibility that witchcraft theorists had to negotiate; the boundary between natural magic and demonic magic was contested and shifting.
- **Evidence**: Neoplatonic/Hermetic tradition; Pomponazzi; early modern natural magic literature.
- **DB relevance**: `natural-demonic-magic-distinction`.

#### Ch. 15 — Demonic Magic (p. 285)
- **Argument**: Natural and demonic magic had parallel intellectual histories — jointly allowed by scholastic knowledge, rising together in the 16th century, falling together when scientific taste changed — demonstrating their shared epistemological ground.
- **Evidence**: Del Rio, parallel texts on natural and demonic causation.
- **DB relevance**: `del-rio-martin-b1551`; `natural-demonic-magic-distinction`.

#### Ch. 16 & 17 — Prerogative Instances 1 & 2 (pp. 306–339)
- **Argument**: Demonological anomalies served as Baconian "prerogative instances" — extreme cases that tested and refined the causal classifications of natural philosophy; studying the demonic was epistemologically productive precisely because it pushed categories to their limits.
- **Evidence**: Bacon's Novum Organum (prerogative instances); Franciscan philosopher Alphonsus de Spina; demonological treatment of lycanthropy, incubi, metamorphosis.
- **DB relevance**: `preternatural-category`; `alphonsus-de-spina` (new entry needed).

#### Ch. 18 — The Magical Power of Signs (p. 340)
- **Argument**: The efficacy of signs — magical words, ritual gestures, amulets — was both a "prerogative instance" for natural philosophy and an arena where demonology collided with controversies over sacramental theology and Neoplatonist semiotics.
- **Evidence**: Controversies over the meaning of language and ritual sign; Protestant/Catholic debate over sacraments.
- **DB relevance**: New concept: `magical-efficacy-of-signs`.

#### Ch. 19 — Witchcraft and the Scientific Revolution (p. 356)
- **Argument**: Demonology was not destroyed by the Scientific Revolution but continued to play a role in late 17th-century natural philosophy because it contributed to the alliance between a particular form of philosophy and a particular form of religion (Boyle, Glanvill, More); its decline followed changes in theological sensibility from the 1740s onward, not prior scientific argument.
- **Evidence**: Glanvill's *Saducismus Triumphatus*; Henry More; Boyle; Royal Society; Bekker's *Betoverde Weereld*.
- **DB relevance**: `glanvill-joseph-b1636`, `bekker-balthasar-b1634`, `more-henry-b1614`; critical revision of Whig history of science narrative.

### Part III: History (Chapters 20–28, pp. 376–515)

#### Ch. 20 — Witchcraft and History (p. 376)
- **Argument**: Witchcraft authors were compelled to address why demonic activity was so intense in their own age; their answers drew on the dominant eschatological historiography of the period, making demonology inseparable from early modern historical thinking.
- **Evidence**: James VI, de Lancre, Franciscus Agricola, Hermann Samson on the intensification of witchcraft in contemporary times.
- **DB relevance**: `james-vi-scotland-b1566`, `de-lancre-pierre-b1553`; new concept: `witchcraft-as-historical-sign`.

#### Ch. 21 — Postremus Furor Satanae (p. 383)
- **Argument**: The idea that contemporary satanic activity was unprecedented — the "final fury of Satan" (postremus furor Satanae) — was framed entirely in eschatological language and linked the prosecution of witches to apocalyptic expectation.
- **Evidence**: Lutheran and Reformed eschatological texts; Reformation preaching.
- **DB relevance**: New concept: `postremus-furor-satanae`.

#### Ch. 22 — Eschatology (p. 399)
- **Argument**: Witchcraft's historical role was determined by biblical prophecy; demonologists read the activities of demons and witches as signs of the last times, applying prophetic exegesis to contemporary events.
- **Evidence**: Biblical apocalypticism (Daniel, Revelation); Protestant and Catholic eschatological commentators.
- **DB relevance**: `witchcraft-as-historical-sign` concept.

#### Ch. 23 — The Life and Times of the Antichrist (p. 413)
- **Argument**: Antichrist theology linked demonology and eschatology in a single schema; the Antichrist's association with magic and false miracles made witchcraft a form of eschatological politics — both Catholic and Protestant writers deployed this, in different ways.
- **Evidence**: Antichrist literature across confessions; Protestant identification of papacy with Antichrist; Catholic counter-arguments.
- **DB relevance**: New concept: `antichrist-demonology-nexus`.

#### Ch. 24 — The Witch as Portent (p. 432)
- **Argument**: Witches were a species of prodigy or "monster" in the early modern culture of wonders; the extensive literature on marvels and prodigies was integrated with demonological thought, making the witch a sign to be read within an eschatological framework.
- **Evidence**: Prodigy literature (Lycosthenes, Boaistuau); demonological overlap with monster literature.
- **DB relevance**: New concept: `witch-as-prodigy-portent`.

#### Ch. 25 — Witch-Cleansing (p. 446)
- **Argument**: Apocalyptic thought gave witch-prosecution a purgative, eschatological urgency — "witch-cleansing" was one preparation for the coming end, expelling Satan's agents before the final events.
- **Evidence**: Eschatological demonological texts; millenarian cleansing discourse.
- **DB relevance**: `diabolical-conspiracy-collective-guilt`.

#### Ch. 26 & 27 — Understanding Possession / Possession, Exorcism, and History (pp. 463–501)
- **Argument**: Demonic possession was intelligible within the same eschatological and inversionary framework as the sabbat; the practice of exorcism acquired historical significance as a re-enactment of Christ's victories over Satan, placing contemporary events in a providential narrative.
- **Evidence**: Possession cases throughout Europe; Catholic/Protestant differences in exorcism practice; the Loudun case (anticipated by two earlier cases in Ch. 28).
- **DB relevance**: New concept: `exorcism-historical-enactment`.

#### Ch. 28 — Before Loudun (p. 502)
- **Argument**: Two pre-Loudun possession cases (detailed) show how eschatological belief became integral to demoniacs' reported behaviour, demonstrating the pattern long before Loudun received historiographical attention.
- **Evidence**: Two specific cases analyzed (names given in text); comparison with Loudun.
- **DB relevance**: Trial events — specific cases (need further reading to extract names).

### Part IV: Religion (Chapters 29–35, pp. 516–645)

#### Ch. 29 — Witchcraft and Religion (p. 516)
- **Argument**: Demonology was not merely saturated with religious values but was inconceivable without them; yet religion in demonological writing was primarily the religiosity of churches — pastoral, evangelical, homiletic — not abstract metaphysics.
- **Evidence**: The enormous output of clerical demonological writing; catechisms, sermons, confessors' manuals; Bacon's identification of witchcraft as "the height of Idolatry."
- **DB relevance**: All demonological scholar entries; `witch-apostasy-heresy-doctrine`.

#### Ch. 30 — Cases of Conscience (p. 525)
- **Argument**: Protestant pastors framed witchcraft affliction primarily as a matter of conscience — a test of faith under divine providence — rather than a crime requiring prosecution; this casuistical approach was derived directly from Reformed theology.
- **Evidence**: Protestant pastoral literature (Perkins, Gifford, Holland, Daneau); casuistry manuals.
- **DB relevance**: `perkins-william-b1558`, `gifford-george-b1548`; concept: `charity-refusal-accusation-model` and `providence-affliction-framework` (new).

#### Ch. 31 — Popular Magic (p. 539)
- **Argument**: Popular magic (cunning folk, healing, divination) was the counterpart against which clerical demonologists defined their concept of illicit magic; the "popular" was not autonomous but constructed relationally in opposition to "learned" norms.
- **Evidence**: Wide range of popular magic practices; Continental and English clerical responses.
- **DB relevance**: `superstition-as-category`; comparison with Hutton's contrary emphasis on cunning folk legitimacy.

#### Ch. 32 — Superstition (p. 557)
- **Argument**: "Superstition" was a technical theological category — defined as reliance on demonic agency for effects — that structured the clerical critique of popular practice; it was not a neutral description but a polemical classification that served Reformation goals.
- **Evidence**: Catholic and Protestant superstition literature; Thomas Aquinas's definition; post-Tridentine and Reformed catechisms.
- **DB relevance**: `superstition-as-category`.

#### Ch. 33 — Reformation (p. 577)
- **Argument**: Reformation dogmatics, casuistry, and catechesis were the institutional matrix that sustained demonological belief; both Protestant and Catholic reformations intensified the conceptual apparatus by which witchcraft was identified and condemned.
- **Evidence**: Doctrinal texts from both confessions; Lutheran, Reformed, and Catholic catechisms; sermons.
- **DB relevance**: `print-culture-demonological-diffusion`, `reformation-demonology-nexus` (new concept).

#### Ch. 34 — Acculturation by Text (p. 602)
- **Argument**: Clark critically engages and partially accepts the "acculturation" thesis (Muchembled, Delumeau) — that Reformation campaigns against popular culture drove witch prosecutions — but insists that demonological texts must be read in their own terms, not only as instruments of elite/popular conflict.
- **Evidence**: Muchembled's thesis; Delumeau's *La Peur*; missionary literature; demonological texts aimed at pastoral reform.
- **DB relevance**: `muchembled-robert-b1944`; critique of social-functionalist readings.

#### Ch. 35 — Protestant Witchcraft, Catholic Witchcraft (p. 623)
- **Argument**: Confessional correlations in witch prosecution are undermined by local variation; nevertheless, there were real differences in the *idiom* of demonological justification between Reformed and Catholic contexts (e.g., the role of the sacraments, attitudes to exorcism).
- **Evidence**: Regional prosecution data; confessional demonological literature.
- **DB relevance**: `protestant-witchcraft-confessional-variation` (new concept).

### Part V: Politics (Chapters 36–44, pp. 646–806)

#### Ch. 36 — Witchcraft and Politics (p. 646)
- **Argument**: The call to prosecute witches was the practical, political culmination of the ideal demonological argument; writers necessarily invoked conceptions of the social order, authority, and justice — making demonology a species of political language.
- **Evidence**: Malleus maleficarum's call for magistrates; Ludwig Milichius, David Meder, Bernhard Albrecht, Lambert Daneau; Romans 13 as key text.
- **DB relevance**: `kramer-heinrich-b1430`, `sprenger-jakob-b1436`; `magistracy-witchcraft-nexus` concept (new).

#### Ch. 37 — Magistrates and Witches (p. 659)
- **Argument**: Appeals to secular magistrates to eradicate witchcraft were not merely incidental but central to demonological argumentation; the magistrate's role was framed in terms of divinely-ordained political authority.
- **Evidence**: Wide survey of Continental and English demonological texts; explicit appeals to magistracy.
- **DB relevance**: Enhances all demonological scholar entries.

#### Ch. 38 — Inviolability (p. 673)
- **Argument**: The idea that magistrates were inviolable from witchcraft — protected by virtue of their divinely-sanctioned office — was both a theological claim and a political one, grounding the magistrate's charismatic authority.
- **Evidence**: Malleus maleficarum; Rémy; Romans 13 as proof text for magistrates' divine protection.
- **DB relevance**: `remy-nicolas-b1530`; new concept: `magistrate-inviolability-doctrine`.

#### Ch. 39 & 40 — The Charisma of Office / Mystical Politics (pp. 686–729)
- **Argument**: Drawing on Weber's typology of authority, Clark argues that the politics invoked by demonologists was "mystical politics" — a sacralized conception of the magistracy that clothed political authority with quasi-sacerdotal charisma.
- **Evidence**: Weber's *Economy and Society*; political theology of magistracy across Europe; divine-right theory.
- **DB relevance**: New concept: `mystical-politics-demonological-frame`.

#### Ch. 41 — Marvellous Monarchy (p. 730)
- **Argument**: "Mystical politics" generated claims about authority that had special kinship with witchcraft arguments: both rested on the logic of oppositions between divine and demonic power, legitimate and illegitimate rule.
- **Evidence**: Divine-right monarchy literature; court festivals; masques.
- **DB relevance**: New concept: `marvellous-monarchy-demonology`.

#### Ch. 42 — Spectacles of Disenchantment (p. 748)
- **Argument**: Spectacular acts of royal power — healing, exorcism, combat with demonic forces — enacted and legitimated charismatic authority; the witch trial itself was a kind of "spectacle of disenchantment" that confirmed the magistrate's sacred power.
- **Evidence**: Royal exorcism; trial spectacles; chivalric combat metaphors.
- **DB relevance**: Enhances `james-vi-scotland-b1566`; new concept: `spectacle-of-disenchantment`.

#### Ch. 43 — Kingcraft and Witchcraft (p. 772)
- **Argument**: Royal healing (the "royal touch") was one of the purest expressions of charismatic authority and was directly connected to the demonological framework; monarchs who healed were performing the opposite of what witches did.
- **Evidence**: Marc Bloch's thaumaturgical healing research; James VI; French and English royal touch.
- **DB relevance**: `james-vi-scotland-b1566`; links to concept of `inversion-principle`.

#### Ch. 44 — Bodin's Political Demonology (p. 789)
- **Argument**: Bodin's *République* (1576) and *Démonomanie* (1580) are not independent works but expressions of a single intellectual vision; the Bodin who theorized sovereignty and the Bodin who advocated witch prosecution were on common ideological ground — between divine right and demonic right there was a natural enmity.
- **Evidence**: Bodin's two major works read in parallel; contemporaries who combined demonological and political absolutism (Pierre Grégoire, Loys Le Caron, Theodor Reinking, Joseph Glanvill).
- **DB relevance**: `bodin-jean-b1530`; direct support for DB entry on Bodin's political demonology.

#### Ch. 45 — Postscript (p. 807)
- **Argument**: This has been a synchronic, not diachronic, study — a thick slice through early modern intellectual life to understand what demonology *was* before attempting to explain its rise or fall; the intellectual positions that constituted demonology (Aristotelian natural philosophy, eschatological historiography, providential religion, mystical politics) each had their own life-histories, and it is their conjunctures that explain the phenomenon.
- **Evidence**: Synthetic statement of book's overall argument; methodological defence of the synchronic approach.
- **DB relevance**: Key passage for `clark-stuart-b1943` DB entry; methodological note for all `scholarly_text` entries.

## Key Passages

### For: `inversion-principle`
> "Witchcraft, Baldung is reminding us, is an act of pure inversion. Witches model their behaviour on our world, just as we do. Because their inspiration is demonic, their perception is overturned; they see and do everything the wrong way up."
*Why this passage matters: The clearest single-sentence statement of Clark's central argument about the sabbat and the inversion principle, grounded in the Baldung Grien analysis.*

### For: `clark-thinking-with-demons-2001.json` (core thesis)
> "I made the further assumption, therefore, that a body of ideas that survived for nearly 300 years must have made some kind of sense and that this probably lay in its coherence with ideas about other things. [...] the subject of witchcraft seems to have been used as a means for thinking through problems that originated elsewhere and that had little or nothing to do with the legal prosecution of witches; hence my adoption of the somewhat Lévi-Straussian title Thinking with Demons to convey this sense of demonology as an intellectual resource."
*Why this passage matters: Clark's clearest statement of the book's methodological premise and the reason for its title — the most important passage for the DB entry abstract.*

### For: `bodin-jean-b1530`
> "In 1576 he published a book that made a decisive impact not only on political theory in France but on discussions of the idea of sovereignty wherever they were later attempted. And in 1580 he published another book whose currency among those interested in witchcraft matters made it the Malleus maleficarum of the next hundred years. [...] the Bodin who urged the judicial destruction of witches was on common ground with the Bodin who reformulated the first principles of absolute sovereignty."
*Why this passage matters: Establishes the necessary connection between Bodin's two major works — crucial for understanding why he appears in both political theory and witchcraft scholarship.*

### For: `trevor-roper-hr-b1914` (new entry)
> "The outstanding modern example is that of Hugh Trevor-Roper who, much as he wished to acknowledge the relativism of Lucien Febvre and the contingent nature of rationality, still dismissed demonology as 'hysterical', 'lunatic', the 'rubbish of the human mind', and 'more bizarre than the psychopathic delusions of the madhouse'."
*Why this passage matters: Clark's dismissal of Trevor-Roper is the clearest marker of his methodological break with an older rationalist historiography — essential for any entry on Trevor-Roper.*

### For: `preternatural-category`
> "From Johannes Nider, Alphonsus de Spina, and Ulrich Molitor to Joseph Glanvill, Balthasar Bekker, and Christian Thomasius these, and a cluster of related questions, were debated over and over again in literally hundreds of texts. [...] the whole dispute centred on whether, as a matter of fact, certain physical events could actually take place in the real world. This meant asking which laws of cause and effect they obeyed, and which they infringed."
*Why this passage matters: Establishes the centrality of the preternatural question — distinguishing natural, preternatural, and supernatural — across the entire history of demonology from the 15th to early 18th century.*

### For: `witches-sabbath-collective`
> "Nicolas Rémy, procureur général of the duchy of Lorraine between 1591 and 1606, wrote typically of the 'preposterous inversion' of the witches' dances and rituals: they love to do everything in a ridiculous and unseemly manner. For they turn their backs towards the Demons when they go to worship them..."
*Why this passage matters: Rémy's account of sabbat inversion is Clark's primary exhibit for the argument that the sabbat was a structured inversion of divine worship, not random or superstitious description.*

### For: `bekker-balthasar-b1634`
> "Demonology continued to play a role in science because it contributed to the alliance 'between one form of philosophy and one form of religion [that] was a dominant feature of late 17th century thought'. It ceased to be relevant to knowledge of nature because of changes in theological sensibilities — which took place from about the 1740s onwards, allowing the breakup of this alliance — not because it was argued out of existence..."
*Why this passage matters: Clark's key argument against the Whig history of science: Bekker's attack on demonic belief was theological, not scientific — important for the Bekker entry's significance field.*
